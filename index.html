Concrete Project Planning Checklist

Planning Your First Concrete Project: A Step by Step Checklist

Most first time concrete jobs do not fail in the mixing or the placing. They fail in the planning: too little concrete ordered, a delivery booked before the formwork is ready, or rain arriving an hour after the pour. This checklist walks through the whole job in the order you should tackle it, so that pour day itself becomes the easiest part.

The short answer: Measure the area and fix the thickness first, because those two numbers decide the volume. Turn the volume into a bag count or a ready mix order, adding 5 to 10 percent for waste. Build the formwork and lay the sub base before anything is ordered, book the concrete for a dry morning with help on hand, and have your curing covers ready before the truck arrives. Work through the five steps below in order and nothing important gets forgotten.

Step 1: Measure the area and choose the thickness

Measure the length and width of the area in metres, checking both ends of each side because garden plots are rarely perfect rectangles. If the two measurements differ, work with the average, or split an awkward shape into simple rectangles and triangles and add the parts together. Sketch the shape on paper with the dimensions written down; you will use this sketch again when ordering and when setting out the formwork.

Thickness depends on what the slab has to carry. The figures below are the standard depths used for domestic work on firm, well compacted ground. Soft or made up ground needs the thicker end of each range and a proper sub base underneath.

Measure the excavation depth as sub base plus slab together. A 100 mm slab on a 100 mm compacted sub base means digging out about 200 mm, plus a little extra so the finished surface sits at the right level against paths and lawns.

Step 2: Work out the volume, then decide bags or ready mix

Volume is simply length multiplied by width multiplied by thickness, all in metres. A patio 4 m by 3 m at 100 mm thick works out at 4 x 3 x 0.1, which is 1.2 cubic metres. Always add 5 to 10 percent on top for uneven digging, spillage and the concrete that stays in the barrow, so this patio really needs about 1.3 cubic metres. Getting this number right matters more than any other decision, and it is worth checking your figures carefully before you order anything, because an order placed on a rough guess is where most budgets go wrong.

The volume then tells you how to buy the concrete. Bagged dry mix suits small jobs: a 20 kg bag yields roughly 0.009 cubic metres, so the patio above would need about 145 bags. A concrete bag calculator works that count out for any size of job, sparing you the pencil maths, and a count that high is slow, heavy work and usually poor value. Ready mix becomes the sensible choice much sooner than most people expect.

Step 4: Ordering, timing and weather

  1. Finish the groundwork first. Excavation, sub base and formwork should all be complete and checked before you book a delivery or buy bags.
  2. Book a morning slot. A morning pour gives you the whole day to place, finish and cover the work, and leaves a margin if the truck runs late.
  3. Check the forecast. Avoid days with heavy rain due, frost expected overnight, or extreme heat. Light cloud and mild temperatures are ideal. Postpone rather than pour into a storm.
  4. Line up enough hands. Placing and screeding is a two person job at minimum; a bigger pour wants three or four, with one person dedicated to the barrow runs.
  5. Confirm the details with the supplier. Give the exact volume including your waste allowance, the mix you need, the site address and any access limits such as narrow gates or a long barrow distance from the road.

Step 5: Have a curing plan before you pour

Concrete gains strength by staying damp while the cement reacts, a process called curing. Plan for it in advance: have polythene sheeting, hessian or curing compound on site before the pour, not in a shopping list for afterwards. Straight after finishing, cover the slab or keep the surface damp, and keep it covered for at least the first few days, longer in hot or windy weather. Keep foot traffic off it for the first couple of days, keep cars off a driveway for at least a week, and protect the surface from frost during the first nights. Most surface problems blamed on bad concrete, dusting, fine cracks and weak edges, actually trace back to the slab drying out too quickly in its first 48 hours.

Frequently asked questions

How much extra concrete should I order?

Add 5 to 10 percent to your calculated volume. Excavations are never perfectly even, some concrete spills, and running short near the end of a pour is far worse than having a barrowful left over. For bagged work, buy the extra bags on a sale or return basis if your merchant offers it.

How accurate do my measurements need to be?

Measure to the nearest centimetre if you can, and measure twice. A 5 cm error on each side of a medium patio changes the volume noticeably. If the shape is irregular, break it into rectangles and triangles and add the parts together rather than guessing one big number.

Can I pour concrete in winter?

Yes, with care. Avoid pouring when frost is expected that night, never place concrete on frozen ground, and protect the fresh slab with insulated covers afterwards. Very cold weather slows curing a lot, so allow extra days before loading the slab.

What is the best day of the week to pour?

A day when you have the full morning free, reliable help available, and at least two dry days following in the forecast. Many people choose a Friday or Saturday so the slab gets an undisturbed weekend of early curing before anyone needs to walk or drive near it.

Do I need reinforcement in a small slab?

Paths and small patios on good ground usually manage without steel. Driveways, garage floors and slabs over filled or soft ground benefit from a layer of welded mesh, which helps control cracking and spreads loads. Keep the mesh near the middle of the slab depth, supported on chairs or spacers rather than lying on the sub base.

When can I remove the formwork?

Side formwork can usually come away after about 24 hours in mild weather, once the edges are hard enough not to crumble. Leave it longer in cold weather, and take the boards off gently so the young edges are not knocked or pried against.

Conclusion

Good concrete work is mostly decided before a single bag is opened. Measure carefully, fix the thickness for the job, turn the volume into an honest order with waste included, and finish every piece of groundwork before anything is booked. Pour on a mild morning with enough help, and have the curing covers ready before the concrete lands. Follow the five steps in order and your first project will look, and last, like the work of someone who has done it before.
